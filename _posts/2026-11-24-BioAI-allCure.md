title: "AI in drug development: what exactly are we accelerating?"

I am optimistic about AI in drug development. I am less convinced by predictions that entire categories of disease will become obsolete within a decade.

Derek Lowe’s recent response to Dario Amodei makes a fair point: confident deadlines for curing disease have been announced before, including by people within medicine. Ambition matters, but a timeline needs more than confidence behind it.[1]

The recent industry discussion adds something useful. As reported by FierceBiotech, Vas Narasimhan distinguishes between information, operational and biological delays in development. We can improve all three, but they present different problems.[2]

For me, the important question is how these gains connect across development. Faster discovery and analysis can help, but their effect on the overall programme depends on experimental validation, operational readiness and how the evidence informs the next decision.

## There is already meaningful progress

Novo Nordisk’s 2024 annual report describes harmonising data from around 1,600 clinical trials to support disease insights, patient stratification and target identification.

It also reports a discovery example combining computational assessment of a billion virtual molecules with laboratory screening of approximately 2,500 compounds, requiring 50–75% fewer design rounds.[3]

Novartis’s November 2026 investor presentation reports approximately 25% faster clinical study report writing and review, and 90% faster statistical programming in selected 2026 trials. Its broader improvements in development time and probability of success are presented as aspirations.[4]

These are company-reported examples, rather than independent evaluations. Nevertheless, they show why it would be wrong to dismiss AI’s contribution.

They also show why we need to be precise about what has improved.

## Faster discovery needs somewhere to go

A candidate identified earlier needs relevant experimental validation. Starting clinical development requires an executable protocol, suitable sites, recruitment capacity, clinical supplies and coordination across teams.

AI can help with these activities. But if discovery accelerates while downstream capacity remains unchanged, the bottleneck may simply move.

The same applies to analysis. Producing results faster is valuable. Interpreting them, challenging assumptions, bringing findings together and communicating uncertainty remain essential. Someone still needs to decide whether the evidence supports advancing, changing or stopping a programme.

Submission preparation offers further opportunities to reduce repetitive work. The evidence must remain traceable, the interpretation defensible and the review accountable.

For me, connecting these activities is central to translating local gains into shorter development timelines.

## Understanding biology and improving outcomes are different achievements

A richer understanding of cells and their interactions is valuable, including when its clinical relevance is not immediately apparent. Fundamental research often creates possibilities we could not have anticipated.

For a therapeutic programme, however, we eventually need to establish whether intervening on a biological process benefits a defined patient population.

A medicine must be manufactured reliably, delivered at an appropriate dose and evaluated for benefit and risk. Shortening discovery does not automatically shorten the exposure or follow-up needed to answer those questions.

Better models may help us choose experiments and design studies more effectively. Their predictions still need evidence appropriate to the decision.

## Different uses require different evaluation

Drug development is also different from helping a clinician make a diagnostic decision. Performance in molecular design does not establish reliability in trial analysis, treatment selection or clinical practice.

A broadly capable model may support several tasks. Each application still needs a clear purpose, suitable data, evaluation and oversight.

The joint FDA–EMA principles for good AI practice reflect this: context of use, multidisciplinary expertise, data governance, proportionate performance assessment and lifecycle management all matter.[5]

I expect lasting value to come from integrating these capabilities into workflows that people can trust and use.

We should measure time saved and costs reduced. We should also ask whether we resolved an important uncertainty, improved a development decision or stopped an unpromising programme earlier.

Those are substantial achievements. Demonstrating them consistently would give us a stronger basis for confidence about what comes next.

*These are my personal views and do not represent my employer.*
